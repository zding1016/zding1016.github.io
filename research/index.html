  1. AI in Disguise—Quasi-Experimental Analysis of a Large-Scale Deployment of AI-Generated Ads
    with Yannick Exner, Jochen Hartmann, Shunyuan Zhang and Oded Netzer
    Marketing Science, 2026
    Abs HTML

    As Taboola prepared to launch its GenAI Ad Maker, a tool that lets advertisers create ad images within the platform, it faced a consequential question about whether AI-generated ad images would perform well with real consumers, and under what conditions. Working with Taboola, we construct a quasi-experimental comparison of 4,633 ’sibling ads,’ meaning ads with AI-generated or human-made images launched by the same advertisers, in identical campaign settings, at the same time. These ads span more than 369 million impressions and 2.5 million clicks. We do not find a detectable average click-through-rate disadvantage for AI-generated images relative to human-made images. However, this average masks an important boundary condition: ads with AI-generated images outperform human-made ads when the AI images do not ’look like AI.’ We identify key visual features influencing consumers’ perceptions of whether an ad looks AI-generated. While AI generates sharper, clearer, and less blurry images with larger faces, consumers associate these features with human-made ads. On the other hand, in line with consumers’ expectations, aesthetics, lower warmth, and intense color saturation in ads signal AI generation. These findings support Taboola’s roll-out communication, advertiser guidance, and continued refinement of the GenAI Ad Maker.

Working Papers

  1. Dynamic Policy Evaluation under Selective Labels: Evidence from Human and Algorithmic Decision-Making in Sequential Allocation
    with Zoey Jiang and Param Vir Singh
    2025
    Working paper
    Abs HTML PDF

    Evaluating sequential decision-making is challenging when outcomes are observed only for chosen actions, creating a selective labels problem that is compounded in dynamic settings where decisions influence future information and learning. This paper studies how to compare human and algorithmic policies in such environments using observational data, focusing on settings where decisions must balance exploration and exploitation over time. We apply this framework to biomedical innovation, modeling scientists’ trial allocation decisions as a combinatorial contextual bandit with delayed feedback using data from over 700,000 experiments in the Protein Structure Initiative. Benchmarking human decisions against a neural upper confidence bound algorithm, we find that the algorithm achieves approximately 18% more successful project completions under comparable constraints. We find that human decision-makers systematically overestimate success probabilities and allocate effort too diffusely across projects, leading to inefficient exploration. Correcting prediction errors accounts for the majority of the performance gap, while improvements in exploration behavior provide additional gains. This paper contributes a general framework for evaluating dynamic policies under selective labels and provides large-scale empirical evidence that algorithmic policies can outperform human experts in complex sequential allocation tasks.
